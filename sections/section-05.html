Spherical Stereo Rectification

To find corresponding points, searching along the epipolar great circles currently requires searching along both the longitude and latitude dimensions. Spherical rectification reduces this to one dimension, making correspondence search faster. The idea is to build a rectifying homography which aligns the pole axis with the baseline.
The effect of this is that the epipoles and the north and south poles (from the pole axis, here y-axis) align. This is good because great circles on a sphere which pass through the north and south poles are called meridians, circles of the same longitude. Thus, one search dimension is eliminated, and corresponding points only differ in their latitude value. Before visualizing the results, let's look at how to build such a rectifying homography:
The new pole axis should point along the stereo baseline. Therefore the first axis is chosen directly from the two camera centers: start at the left camera center and point toward the right camera center. After normalization this becomes the new y-axis. \[ \textcolor{#00A650}{ \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{new}} = \frac{\mathbf{C}_R - \mathbf{C}_L} {\left\lVert \mathbf{C}_R - \mathbf{C}_L \right\rVert}} \]
To choose a stable forward direction, first look at the old z-axes of both cameras. They describe where the left and right cameras were looking before rectification. \[ \textcolor{#CCCCFF}{\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{old}}_L}, \qquad \textcolor{#CCCCFF}{\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{old}}_R} \]
Move those two old viewing directions together. Their sum gives a single average viewing direction for the rectified pair. \[ \textcolor{#CCCCFF}{ \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{temp}} = \mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{old}}_L + \mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{old}}_R} \]
This temporary vector is useful, but it is not yet perpendicular to \(\textcolor{#00A650}{\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{new}}}\). The dashed line shows the part of \(\textcolor{#CCCCFF}{\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{temp}}}\) that lies in the direction of the new y-axis.
Gram-Schmidt now measures exactly how much of \(\textcolor{#CCCCFF}{\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{temp}}}\) points along \(\textcolor{#00A650}{\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{new}}}\). \[ \left\langle \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{temp}}, \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{new}} \right\rangle \]
Multiplying this scalar with the direction \(\textcolor{#00A650}{\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{new}}}\) gives the full projection vector. This is the part that must be removed from \(\textcolor{#CCCCFF}{\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{temp}}}\). \[ \textcolor{#FF1493}{ \left\langle \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{temp}}, \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{new}} \right\rangle \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{new}}} \]
Subtract the projection from the temporary vector. What remains is perpendicular to \(\textcolor{#00A650}{\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{new}}}\), so it can become the new z-axis after normalization. Or in other words "If \( \textcolor{#FF1493}{\bullet} + \textcolor{blue}{\bullet} = \textcolor{#CCCCFF}{\bullet} \) then \( \textcolor{blue}{\bullet} = \textcolor{#CCCCFF}{\bullet} - \textcolor{#FF1493}{\bullet} \)" \[ \textcolor{#0000FF}{ \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}} = \frac{ \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{temp}} - \left\langle \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{temp}}, \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{new}} \right\rangle \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{new}} } { \left\lVert \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{temp}} - \left\langle \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{temp}}, \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{new}} \right\rangle \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{new}} \right\rVert }} \]
Now the rectified coordinate frame already has two orthonormal axes: \(\textcolor{#00A650}{\mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{new}}}\) along the baseline and \(\textcolor{#0000FF}{\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}}}\) as the corrected forward direction.
The last axis is fixed by the right-handed coordinate system. Take the cross product of the two axes that are already known: \[ \textcolor{#FF0000}{ \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}} = \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{new}} \times \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}}} \] Together, these three axes form the rotation used in the rectifying homography.
Now the three new axes are stacked into the rectifying rotation. Each row tells where one axis of the new camera frame points in the old coordinate system: \[ \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{rect}} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}}^T \\ \mathbf{y}_{\mathrm{new}}^T \\ \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{new}}^T \end{bmatrix} \] This does not change the real cameras. Instead, it creates a virtual rectified camera coordinate system and rotates both camera views into that same virtual frame. To be more specific, if the original camera extrinsic rotations are \( \mathbf{R}_L, \mathbf{R}_R \) and \(\mathbf{d}_L\) and \(\mathbf{d}_R\) are some viewing ray directions of a pixel in the left and right camera coordinate systems, then each ray can be mapped into the shared rectified frame like this: \[ \mathbf{d}_{L,\mathrm{rect}} = \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{rect}} \mathbf{R}_L^T \mathbf{d}_L \] \[ \mathbf{d}_{R,\mathrm{rect}} = \mathbf{R}_{\mathrm{rect}} \mathbf{R}_R^T \mathbf{d}_R \] With these new rectified ray directions, a new rectified equirectangular projection can be built for the left and right images. As mentioned above, the epipole axis is now aligned with the pole axis and epipolar great circles become meridians, meaning circles of equal longitude.
