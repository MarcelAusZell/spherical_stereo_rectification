Spherical Epipolar Geometry

Before continuing with the relationship of correspondences between two fisheye images, it is important to note that some of those directions stand out. For example, the center pixel of the equirectangular projection corresponds to the forward direction \[ (u_{\mathrm{Longitude}},v_{\mathrm{Latitude}}) = \textcolor{#7878FF}{(0,0)} \Rightarrow \begin{bmatrix} \sin(\mathrm{longitude})\cos(\mathrm{latitude}) \\ \sin(\mathrm{latitude}) \\ \cos(\mathrm{longitude})\cos(\mathrm{latitude}) \end{bmatrix} = \textcolor{#7878FF}{ \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 0\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}} \]
Depending on the chosen spherical parameterization and the selected longitude and latitude ranges, these directions may differ. What remains invariant, though, is the existence of an optical axis, a pole axis and a third remaining axis orthogonal to the previous two.
Given these coordinate conventions, the epipolar geometry between two spherical cameras can be established:

Consider two cameras observing a world point \(P\). The projections of \(P\) onto the unit spheres of the left and right cameras are denoted by \(s_L\) and \(s_R\), respectively. The cameras themselves are described by their centers \(C_L\) and \(C_R\) as well as their orientations in the world coordinate system. In the present convention, the optical axes point approximately towards the observed scene, while the pole axes define the north and south poles of the spherical images. Furthermore, the baseline connecting the two camera centers intersects each unit sphere in two antipodal points. These points describe the epipoles of the corresponding camera and represent the directions towards and away from the opposite camera center.

If the world point \(P\) is seen by camera \(R\) as \(P_R\) and by camera \(L\) as \(P_L\), then their relationship can be described by a rigid body transformation: \[ \begin{align*} && P_R &= R P_L + t \\ \iff && \lambda_R s_R &= R \lambda_L s_L + t && \left(\substack{{P_R = \lambda_R s_R} \\ {P_L = \lambda_L s_L}}\right) \\ \iff && \lambda_R t \times s_R &= t \times R \lambda_L s_L + \underbrace{t \times t}_{=0} && \left(\substack{\text{Cross product from left}}\right)\\ \iff && \underbrace{s_R \cdot ( \lambda_R t \times s_R)}_{=0} &= s_R \cdot (t \times R \lambda_L s_L) && \left(\substack{\text{Dot product from left}}\right)\\ \iff && 0 &= \lambda_L s_R \cdot (t \times R s_L) \\ \iff && 0 &= s_R \cdot (t \times R s_L) && \left(\substack{\lambda_L \neq 0}\right)\\ \iff && 0 &= s_R^\top (t \times R s_L) && \left(\substack{a \cdot b \,=\, a^\top b}\right)\\ \iff && 0 &= s_R^\top ([t]_\times R s_L) && \left(\substack{\text{Cross product as skew symmetric matrix}}\right)\\ \iff && 0 &= s_R^\top E s_L && \left(\substack{E = [t]_\times R}\right) \end{align*} \] Which is exactly the same as the epipolar constraint in classic stereo vision. But observe now that this epipolar constraint does not enforce an epipolar line (as in the planar stereo setup) but an epipolar great circle given by \[ \begin{align*} G_R(s_L) &= \{s \in \mathbb{S}^2 \mid s^\top \underbrace{E s_L}_{n_R} = 0\} \\ &= \{s \in \mathbb{S}^2 \mid s^\top n_R = 0\} \\ \end{align*} \] The vector \( n_R = E s_L \) defines the normal of the epipolar plane (as seen by camera \(C_R\)). Consequently, all corresponding viewing directions \(s\) satisfy \( s^\top n_R = 0 \) which is another way of saying that they lie within this plane. Thus the intersection of the epipolar plane with the sphere forms a great circle, which represents the spherical epipolar curve, also called the epipolar great circle:
