Fisheye Projections

For this reason, fisheye images are often transformed into alternative image representations that simplify further processing. A common choice is the equirectangular projection. An equirectangular image can be understood as a flattened representation of a sphere, similar to how world maps represent the surface of the Earth. The horizontal axis corresponds to the longitude, while the vertical axis corresponds to the latitude. Since the longitude covers a range of \(360^\circ\) and the latitude a range of \(180^\circ\), equirectangular images typically use a width-to-height ratio of 2:1. Starting with a regular image grid, each pixel position is interpreted as a longitude and latitude pair. The longitude covers the range from \(-\pi\) to \(\pi\), while the latitude covers the range from \(-\frac{\pi}{2}\) to \(\frac{\pi}{2}\). \[ \begin{align*} u \in [0,W) &\overset{/W}{\longrightarrow} \frac{u}{W} \in [0,1) \\ &\overset{\cdot\,2\pi}{\longrightarrow} \frac{u}{W}\,2\pi \in [0,2\pi) \\ &\overset{-\pi}{\longrightarrow} \left(\frac{u}{W}\,2\pi\right)-\pi \in [-\pi,\pi) \end{align*} \begin{align*} v \in [0,H) &\overset{/H}{\longrightarrow} \frac{v}{H} \in [0,1) \\ &\overset{\cdot\,\pi}{\longrightarrow} \frac{v}{H}\,\pi \in [0,\pi) \\ &\overset{-\frac{\pi}{2}}{\longrightarrow} \left(\frac{v}{H}\,\pi\right)-\frac{\pi}{2} \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right) \end{align*} \]
These angle pairs define viewing directions on the unit sphere. Using spherical coordinates, each longitude and latitude pair can be converted into a three-dimensional unit vector: \[ \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \sin(\mathrm{longitude}) \cos(\mathrm{latitude}) \\ \sin(\mathrm{latitude}) \\ \cos(\mathrm{longitude}) \cos(\mathrm{latitude}) \end{bmatrix} \]
These directions can then be projected into the original fisheye image using the calibrated camera model. This produces image coordinates \((u,v)\) in the fisheye image.
Fisheye Camera Models
Since we are dealing with fisheye images, a fisheye camera model is typically used. Such models try to imitate the behavior of a physical fisheye lens. Conventional pinhole cameras can only observe a limited field of view. Rays arriving from very large viewing angles would intersect the image plane outside the sensor and therefore could not be captured. Real fisheye lenses overcome this limitation by using a combination of convex and concave lens elements that bend incoming rays toward the image center. Accurately modeling the complete optical system would be very complicated and often unnecessary. Instead, camera models provide a simplified mathematical description of this behavior. The equidistant model, for example, assumes that equal angular distances on the viewing sphere correspond to equal distances on the image plane, thus creating a "pulling effect" of the ray towards the optical center

If you will you can also imagine the equirectangular image as a texture wrapped around a sphere, similar to a sticker covering a globe. Each pixel of the image corresponds to exactly one viewing direction on the unit sphere. The field of view of a camera thereby just determines how much of this sphere is visible. A conventional camera may only observe a relatively small region of the sphere, whereas fisheye cameras can capture a much larger portion, often approaching or even exceeding \(180^\circ\).
